There is a guitar player in Nashville who can hear things you can't.

I don't mean that as flattery. I mean it the way you'd say a sommelier can taste things you can't, or a radiologist can see things you can't in the gray smear of an X-ray. His name is Jim Lill, and he makes videos on the internet about how music actually works — not how it's supposed to work, not how the magazines say it works, but how it works when you put it on a bench and start unscrewing the back panel. He is a session musician, which means people pay him to play guitar correctly, on the first take, in tune, in time, with no excuses. That is a real job and a hard one. It selects for a particular kind of ear. The kind that notices.

I want to tell you about something Jim did recently, and why I've been arguing with it in my head for a week. But first I have to tell you why I trust him, because the argument doesn't mean anything if you think I'm just picking a fight.

So. Three things.

The first is that he runs experiments. Actual ones. There's a video where he sets out to answer a question that the entire guitar industry has spent sixty years being deliberately vague about: where, physically, does the sound of an amplifier come from? Is it the tubes? The transformer? The speaker? The wood of the cabinet, the way the brochures always imply, with their reverent photographs of hand-soldered circuits? Jim does the obvious thing that almost nobody does, which is to test it. He swaps the parts one at a time and listens. He isolates variables like a chemist. And he arrives at an answer that makes a lot of expensive mythology look silly. I'm not going to spoil the whole thing, but I'll say that I respect a man who would rather measure than genuflect.

The second thing is harder to value and more valuable. Jim listens to music that isn't his. He came up in country, in and around the Nashville machine, with the trained and slightly merciless ear that world installs in you. And instead of staying in that lane, he goes and sits with records from genres and decades he doesn't know — bands he's heard of, has maybe caught a single from, but has never actually listened to closely, the way you'd listen if you were going to have to play it back. Then he plays it back. He reports what he hears. The outsider's ear is a genuine instrument, and almost nobody is brave enough to use it in public, because using it in public means admitting you don't already know.

The third thing is that he says what he thinks. Bluntly. Without the protective fog that most people lay down over an opinion so that no one can pin them to it later. This matters more than it looks, and I'll come back to it, because it's also the thing that I think trips him up.

Now here is what Jim did. He sat down and deep-listened to Led Zeppelin — not as a fan, not as a hater, but as that radiologist, the man reading the X-ray. He pulled the tracks apart. He listened to Jimmy Page's guitar the way you'd listen to a session player you were thinking about hiring. And his verdict, boiled down to its hard center, was something close to this: I don't really get it. The playing is loose. The pitch wanders. The timing is approximate. There are notes in these famous recordings that, if I played them on a paid session, would get me a polite phone call and no second booking. So what we are calling greatness here — what the whole culture has agreed to call one of the greatest rock bands that ever lived — is, when you actually measure it, mostly marketing. Mostly a story we were sold. Point to the thing. Point to the actual sound coming out of the actual speaker that justifies the legend. He listened for it, carefully, and he couldn't find it.

I love this band. I should put that on the table. I came up a little after them, a kid in the late eighties and early nineties whose defining records were really Pearl Jam and the radio-rock of that moment, but Zeppelin was in the water, the way Pink Floyd was in the water, and I love them now without irony. So I have a dog in this fight. Fine. But that's not why I disagree with Jim, and I want to be careful here, because the easy move — the move that would get me likes and would also be intellectually worthless — is to call him a philistine who can't feel the magic, and ride off feeling superior.

I'm not going to do that. Jim isn't wrong about what he heard. The pitch does wander. The timing is approximate. He has better ears than I do and he is reporting, honestly, what those ears picked up. The problem isn't his measurement. The problem is much older and much deeper than Led Zeppelin, and it's the same problem that once made it career suicide for a scientist to suggest that a dog might have an inner life. The problem is what we have decided counts as evidence — and, underneath that, the much more dangerous belief that some of us are standing outside our own culture, reading the dial clean, while everyone else is lost in a fog of preference and nostalgia and good marketing.

That belief is the thing I want to take apart. Not Jim. The belief.

Because here is the part that should worry you, whatever you think about a rock band from the seventies: the most cultured person in any room is usually the one most certain he has no culture. The one who says, calmly, your taste is shaped by where you grew up and what got played on your five local radio stations and what your friends thought was cool — but mine isn't. Mine is just accurate. I'm not telling you what I like. I'm telling you what's good.

Hold onto that sentence.

That's where the opening ends and the real argument begins — through the physics of why two notes ring together, the decades when science forbade itself from asking whether a dog has an inner life, the blue note that's only "wrong" inside a frame, and what a guitar-amp plug-in I named after Jim has to do with any of it.
