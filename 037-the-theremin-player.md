It all goes back to Maggie Gyllenhaal. Not the folktronica sessions, not the daily discipline of showing up to the studio, but the deeper question of what it means to commit to a sound you have never made before. In the 2014 film Frank, Gyllenhaal plays Clara, the theremin player in an avant-garde band led by a masked genius. She initially turned down the role. The script made no sense to her on first read. But something about it stuck, and weeks later she called back and said yes. Then she actually learned to play the theremin.

The theremin is the strangest instrument in standard orchestral use. Invented by Léon Theremin in 1920, it is played without physical contact. The performer moves their hands through electromagnetic fields generated by two antennas. One hand controls pitch, the other controls volume. It looks like magic. It sounds like a ghost singing through a radio. Most musicians never touch one. Gyllenhaal had her husband find her a teacher, and she learned it for a role in a film that would run ninety-five minutes and be seen by a few thousand people in art house theaters.

This is the commitment that the producer makes when they sit down with the blank session. Not the commitment to finish, though that matters. The commitment to learn something new in service of the song. Clara in Frank is protective, aggressive, uncompromising. She plays the theremin and a Korg MS-20 synthesizer. She is the gatekeeper of the band's sound, suspicious of the newcomer Jon who wants to post their sessions on YouTube and make them famous. The film asks whether exposure helps or harms the creative process. Whether the act of sharing the work corrupts the work itself.

Frank was directed by Lenny Abrahamson, an Irish filmmaker drawn to characters who do not fit. The title character wears a papier-mâché mask at all times, played by Michael Fassbender who never removed it during filming. The mask was inspired by Frank Sidebottom, the comic persona of British musician Chris Sievey, but the film is fictional. It draws from other outsider musicians: Daniel Johnston, who recorded lo-fi songs while struggling with schizophrenia; Captain Beefheart, whose Trout Mask Replica remains one of the most challenging albums in rock history; Roky Erickson, the psychedelic pioneer whose career was interrupted by mental illness.

The film premiered at Sundance in January 2014 and holds 92 percent on Rotten Tomatoes. It won the British Independent Film Award for Best Screenplay. Jon Ronson, who co-wrote the script, had actually toured with Frank Sidebottom in the 1980s. He and co-writer Peter Straughan chose to fictionalize the story rather than make a biopic. Chris Sievey himself did not want a biographical film. He gave his blessing to a story that used the mask as a starting point but went somewhere else entirely.

That somewhere else is a question about creativity and mental illness. In the film, Jon assumes that Frank's genius comes from his suffering. That the mask hides trauma. That great art requires a broken artist. The film's final act rejects this. Frank's parents explain that he was always musical. If anything, his illness slowed him down. The torment did not make the music. This is an uncomfortable truth for the producer who romanticizes the struggle. The work is separate from the suffering. The sound exists on its own terms.

Clara knows this. She plays the theremin because it makes a sound that cannot be made any other way. Not because she is damaged. Not because she needs to prove something. She learned the instrument for a role and kept the knowledge. This is what the daily session requires. The willingness to learn the theremin even when no one is watching. Even when the film will run ninety-five minutes and be seen by a few thousand people. Even when the song might never leave the studio.

The theremin player in Frank is the gatekeeper of authenticity. She is suspicious of Jon's social media posts. She does not want the band's process commodified. She is right to be suspicious, but she is also wrong. The film does not take a side. It shows the tension and leaves it unresolved. The producer in 2026 faces the same tension. Every upload is a performance. Every session is both private and public. The theremin player knows that the sound matters more than the audience. The producer must decide whether they agree.

Gyllenhaal's commitment to learning the theremin is the model. She did not fake it. She did not mime to a prerecorded track. She learned an instrument that she had never played for a role that she almost turned down. This is the level of specificity that the session demands. Not the theremin itself, but the willingness to learn it. The willingness to make a sound that has never been made before. The willingness to sit with the discomfort of not knowing how something will turn out.

It all goes back to Maggie Gyllenhaal. Not because she is the answer, but because she asked the question. What does it mean to commit to a sound? What does it mean to learn something new in service of the work? What does it mean to show up every day and make something that might not be understood? The theremin player knows. The producer is learning.
