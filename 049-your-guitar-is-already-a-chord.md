So here's the thing I kept thinking about while making this one. You play one note on a guitar — one string, one pitch — and you think you're hearing one note. You're not. You're hearing a stack.

When a string vibrates, it doesn't just vibrate at the pitch you played. It also vibrates at twice that frequency, and three times, and four times, on up the line. Those are the overtones, the harmonic series, and they're all sounding at once, quietly, on top of the note you think is the note. The lowest one, the fundamental, is the pitch you name. Everything above it is the reason a guitar sounds like a guitar and not like a flute playing the same note. The pitch tells you which note. The overtones tell you what it is. That recipe — how loud each overtone is relative to the others — is basically the fingerprint of an instrument.

And the recipe isn't random. There's a split running right through it. The even-numbered overtones — the second, the fourth, the sixth — line up with octaves and the friendly intervals on top of them, so they tend to sound sweet and blended, like they belong. The odd ones — the third, the fifth, the seventh — are the ones that give you that hollow, reedy, slightly hard edge. My favorite proof of this is the clarinet. A clarinet is basically a tube closed at one end, and the physics of that shape means it mostly produces odd harmonics and skips the even ones. That's the whole reason it sounds hollow and woody instead of round. Same notes as a flute, totally different character, and a big chunk of the difference is just: which overtones got to show up.

Okay. So that's the idea I built the whole track around, because Bitwig has a device that lets you literally pull that stack apart. It's called Harmonic Split, and it's part of the Spectral Suite — these effects that work in the frequency domain instead of the normal in-and-out of a waveform. Most plugins treat a sound as one moving signal. The spectral stuff chops it into hundreds of little frequency bands and lets you sort them. Harmonic Split is the one that sorts by the overtone series. It listens to the incoming sound, figures out the fundamental, calculates where all the harmonics should be, and then splits them onto separate outputs. By default it sends the odd harmonics to one channel and the even ones to another. And then there's a third channel for everything that doesn't fit the math — the pick noise, the finger squeaks, the breathy inharmonic stuff that isn't part of the clean overtone series. So you take one guitar and you get, in effect, three guitars: the hollow one, the sweet one, and the noisy one.

Once a sound is in pieces like that, the obvious thing is to treat the pieces differently. Pan them apart. Put reverb on only the even harmonics so the sweet part floats back and the body stays dry and close. Distort the odd ones to make them bite. But the move I actually liked in this session was simpler and a little weirder. I wanted the guitar to drift between its two halves. Not a hard switch — a slow morph, the timbre quietly breathing from hollow to sweet and back without a single note ever changing.

The way I got there was with Audio Receivers. An Audio Receiver is a little Bitwig device whose only job is to grab a signal from somewhere else in the project and drop it into wherever it's sitting. It's a patch cable, basically. So I put the split channels into reach of receivers on another track, and used the balance between them as a kind of homemade mix knob — turn it one way, you're hearing the odd-harmonic version, turn it the other way, the even one. And then I didn't turn it by hand. I handed that knob to an LFO. A low, slow one, way under a cycle a second, just gently rocking the balance back and forth on its own. That's the whole trick. The arpeggios stay exactly the same notes the entire time, but the instrument keeps shifting underneath them, like the guitar can't decide what it is, and the indecision is the part that's nice to listen to.

I should be honest that this is a finicky thing to set up. The fundamental detection has a sensitivity to it, and on a busy chord it can get confused about what the root even is, so the cleaner and more spaced-out the arpeggio, the better the split behaves. That's actually why the guitar part is so sparse and arpeggiated up top — it's not just an aesthetic call, it's giving the device a fighting chance to hear the overtones clearly. Everything else on the track came after, just to fill the room out: a bit of bass underneath, a high little hi-hat for some pulse, and a drifting banjo lead floating over the top. Dressing. The harmonic split is the actual subject.

What I keep coming back to is that none of this is adding anything. It's all already in the string. The hollow guitar and the sweet guitar were both in there the whole time, folded together into the one sound you call a note, and the device just unfolds them so you can hear them as separate things and play one against the other. You're not building a chord. You're noticing the one that was already there.

Anyway. That's the track. I'll probably keep poking at this approach — there's a lot left in those three channels I didn't use. Thanks for hanging out.
