Joe Meek pointed a microphone at a grave and heard something answer. This was the late nineteen-fifties, in an English churchyard, and the something he heard he eventually decided was the voice of Buddy Holly, who was not yet dead. Meek built the first home recording studio in Britain that produced anything that mattered, a cramped warren of rooms above a leather-goods shop on the Holloway Road in north London, and from inside it he made "Telstar," an instrumental that went to number one on both sides of the Atlantic and sold something like five million copies, a piece of music that still sounds like a transmission beamed in from a future that has since been quietly cancelled. He was self-taught, working-class, and homosexual at a moment when that was a criminal offence he was in fact arrested for, and he was hearing the dead through tape hiss before most of his countrymen had heard a record in stereo. He kept dragging tape machines out to graveyards. He came to believe that Holly was contacting him from the spirit world, and when Holly died in a plane crash on the third of February, nineteen fifty-nine, Meek took it not as grief but as proof, confirmation that the machine had been telling him the truth all along. Eight years later, to the day, on the third of February, nineteen sixty-seven, swallowed by amphetamine paranoia and the conviction that rival producers were stealing his ideas through the walls, Meek loaded a single-barrelled shotgun, killed his landlady Violet Shenton on the staircase, and then killed himself. The matching of the date was not, to him, a coincidence. By then nothing was a coincidence to him. That is the first thing to understand about the pattern, before we have even agreed that there is one: the people who live inside it do not experience it as a pattern. They experience it as a message addressed, specifically and only, to them.

I want to ask the question the culture has trained us not to ask, because the trained answer is so flattering and so cheap that asking the real question feels like bad manners at a funeral. Why does a particular kind of songwriter keep arriving, by a hundred different roads, at the same small room? I mean the intimate ones, the harmonically strange ones, the ones allergic to the professional's polished mask, the kind we have agreed to file under "folk" even when there is an entire amplified band roaring behind them, because what makes them folk is not the instrumentation but a refusal: the insistence that the song come straight off the nerve with nothing in between. There is a story we tell about these people and it goes like this: the suffering produced the beauty, the wound was the price of the gift, and we, the listeners, are the lucky inheritors of a transaction we did not have to pay for. This story is a lie, and it is worse than a lie, because it converts a recurring catastrophe into a kind of cosmic patronage scheme, and it lets everyone who is not dead off the hook. But the opposite story, the clinical one, is also a lie. It says these were nine unrelated men with nine unrelated illnesses and the music was incidental, a symptom we happen to find pretty, and that there is no pattern at all, only the human eye's hunger to draw a line through scattered points. I do not believe that either. I think there is a pattern and I think it is real and I think we do not have a vocabulary honest enough to name it, so we reach for the three explanations we keep in the drawer. We say it is in the genes, a heritable predisposition that would have detonated whether or not the man ever picked up a guitar. We say it is the trauma, the specific wound, the response of a damaged organism to an injury that came from outside. Or we say, more vaguely and more uneasily, that it is something structural, a process larger than any single life that simply uses these particular lives as the place where it becomes briefly audible. Hold those three in your hands. We are going to need all of them, and none of them is going to be enough.

Begin with Jackson C. Frank, because with Frank you can touch the wound; it left scars you could photograph. In nineteen fifty-four, in Cheektowaga, New York, a furnace exploded under the annex of his elementary school during a music lesson and killed fifteen of his eleven-year-old classmates. Frank survived with burns across half his body, and during the months he spent in the burn ward learning, slowly, how to live inside ruined skin, somebody handed him a guitar. When the insurance settlement came he used it to buy passage on a boat to England, and there, in nineteen sixty-five, with Paul Simon producing, he recorded the ten songs of his only album in under three hours. He was so ashamed of his scarred body that he made them erect screens around him in the studio, so that no one could watch him sing "Blues Run the Game." Then the structure closed over him: a diagnosis of schizophrenia he rejected for the rest of his life, insisting instead that what he had was depression caused by the fire, the trauma, the wound — which is to say that the patient himself reached into the drawer and chose explanation number two over explanation number one. Homelessness followed, and then, sitting on a New York street, he was shot in the eye, by a stray bullet or by nothing that made sense, and he died in a nursing home in nineteen ninety-nine, the day after his birthday. With Frank the trauma is not a metaphor. It is a date and a death toll and a percentage of skin.

With Syd Barrett the wound goes invisible and the explanation slides one slot over. Barrett wrote the songs that made Pink Floyd, sang "Astronomy Domine" as though he were narrating his own departure from the planet, and then, somewhere in the acid year of nineteen sixty-seven, the lights behind his eyes changed. He would detune his guitar onstage until it produced nothing, or stand and not play at all, staring out. By nineteen sixty-eight the band had effectively left him on the pavement, picking up another guitarist and driving past. He made two solo records that are not records of madness so much as recordings of a mind coming apart in real time, audibly, take by take, and then he went home to Cambridge, to his mother's house, became "Roger" again, painted, gardened, grew heavy and bald and silent, and lived another forty years while the band he founded sold tens of millions of albums, several of them explicitly about him, about the brilliant boy who left. Was it the drugs or was it always coming, lit fuse or loaded gene? Nobody can say, and the fact that nobody can say is not a gap in our knowledge. It is the thing itself.

Then there is the matter of sheer volume, which is its own kind of symptom, and here you turn to Daniel Johnston, who made something past a hundred and fifty songs and dubbed many of them one cassette at a time on a boombox, handing them to strangers in Austin. Manic-depressive, later schizophrenic, institutionalized again and again, he wrote "True Love Will Find You in the End," a song of total and unbearable faith sung by a man for whom true love demonstrably did not, fixating for decades on a woman named Laurie he had known briefly in art school. A major-label bidding war was conducted over him while he was locked in a hospital. The directness is the disease and the disease is the directness; you cannot get a blade between them. He sang as if the membrane the rest of us keep between the inner life and the open mouth had simply never grown over him, and you can hear, in the flood of it, the cost of having no skin.

Jeff Mangum had skin, briefly, and then the success of using it destroyed him. In nineteen ninety-eight, with Neutral Milk Hotel, he made "In the Aeroplane Over the Sea," an album so flooded with grief — much of it for Anne Frank, a dead girl he had never met and could not stop mourning — that he could not survive its being loved. The more people pressed it to their chests, the further he withdrew, until the withdrawal itself became the legend and the legend fed the withdrawal, a feedback loop of absence. The pattern does not require death. Sometimes it only requires that the door close and stay closed.

Carry that forward to the most recent instance and the one still raw, because Isaac Wood is alive, and you must hold onto that as you read the rest. With Black Country, New Road he made "The Place Where He Inserted the Blade," and then, four days before the band released their second album in early twenty twenty-two, Wood left. The statement he gave was almost impossible to read for how plainly it was written: it was, he said, the kind of sad and afraid feeling that made it hard to play guitar and sing at the same time. The band continued without him and, out of something better than loyalty, stopped performing his songs entirely. He walked away from the exact threshold the others crossed. The door was open and he turned around. We do not know what that costs or what it buys, but it is the one variation in the pattern that should make us least, not most, certain that the pattern is fixed.

Nick Drake recorded "Pink Moon" almost alone across a couple of nights, twenty-eight minutes of a man subtracting himself from his own arrangements, and three commercially invisible albums later he was dead at twenty-six in nineteen seventy-four, an overdose of the antidepressant amitriptyline that no coroner could rule deliberate or accidental. Elliott Smith double-tracked his voice into a whisper on "Between the Bars" until it was almost too intimate to remain in the room with, carried his addiction through an Oscar nomination he performed like a man held at gunpoint, and died in two thousand and three of two stab wounds to the chest that were ruled neither suicide nor homicide, a question the state of California formally declined to answer. Jason Molina, who as Songs: Ohia and Magnolia Electric Co. sang in the flattened vowels of the rust belt, titled his masterpiece "Farewell Transmission" — a transmission, a signal sent precisely as the sender vanishes, which is the same metaphor Joe Meek had been living inside with his graveyard microphones, the dead voice on the tape, the message from the far side of the carrier wave. Molina drank for four years and his organs failed and he died in twenty thirteen at thirty-nine. And David Berman, who made the Silver Jews and then, after a decade of silence, returned in twenty nineteen under the name Purple Mountains with "That's Just the Way That I Feel," a record so undefended about depression that it reads in hindsight as a note left on the table, and who hanged himself weeks after its release and days before the tour was to begin. Berman's father was Rick Berman, the lobbyist who built a career representing the alcohol and tobacco and firearms industries and attacking labour unions, a man who is something close to a pure distillation of the machine his son spent his entire life singing against. With Berman the three explanations stop being alternatives and start to braid. The structure is not only in the culture and not only in the chemistry. It is in the bloodline, sitting at the head of the family table.

So look at what we have, nine men and the engineer who came before them, and ask the question with the manners stripped off. Genetics will tell you that what recurs here is heritable load, that the songs are foam thrown up by a wave of predisposition, and the genetics is not wrong, only radically incomplete, because it cannot explain why the load expresses itself, again and again, through this specific vocation, the one built on the refusal to wear the professional's mask. Trauma theory will tell you, with Frank as its star witness, that each was a wounded organism behaving lawfully, and that too is true and too small, because it treats the culture that prizes and purchases the wound as weather rather than as agent. And here the structural account, the one we are most reluctant to say out loud, becomes unavoidable. The position these men occupied — total exposure, the nerve held up to the open air, the self offered without the mediating armour that lets the rest of us survive being looked at — is precisely the position that the market cannot sustain and the psyche cannot defend, and we know this, and we keep sending people to occupy it because the recordings they send back from inside it are the most valuable thing we have. Baruch Spinoza, three and a half centuries ago, wrote that no one yet has determined what a body can do, and in the same breath dismantled the comforting fiction of free will, arguing that we are entirely determined and that the only freedom available to a human being is the adequate understanding of how, exactly, we are determined. The post-structuralist thinkers who came after him took the dead and put them at the centre: Jacques Derrida coined the word hauntology to describe a present that is constituted by the ghosts of futures that never arrived, and there is no purer instance of it than a recorded voice, which is always, already, the voice of someone who is in the act of disappearing, every record a séance, every playback Joe Meek's microphone in the churchyard catching what the living were never supposed to hear. When Molina titled the song "Farewell Transmission" he was not being morbid. He was being accurate. The recording is the part of you that goes on transmitting after the body that made it has been switched off, and some part of every one of these men seems to have understood, while making it, that this was the actual function of the work and the actual cost.

Which returns us, finally, to the only question that was ever worth asking, the one the suffering-equals-beauty story exists specifically to prevent us from asking. Did they choose? Did Berman choose, with his father's machine running in his own blood; did Barrett choose, with whatever was always going to switch on switching on; did any of them author the undoing that we have agreed to call their art, or were they reading aloud, with great feeling, from a script written before they were born — in the genes, in the wound, in the structure that needs the nerve held up to the air and will always find someone willing to hold it? And the harder version, the one that should keep you awake, is not about them at all.
