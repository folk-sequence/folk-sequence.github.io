I have always been cautious about calling myself a musician or composer or producer or mixer. Those titles feel like they belong to people who can sustain a full performance, who can hold a room for an hour without the anxiety becoming visible. I am comfortable with songwriter, lyricist, even to some extent vocal performer, and I know enough guitar—mainly hitting the one beat reliably—to make for a compelling one person show. But I like to keep it at a couple of songs. That gives me a serious rush, a glow. Longer performances, while exhilarating, have also been taxing, draining and anxiety-ridden. So I control my dosage.

It occurs to me lately, and especially during this fifteen minute session, that one of the skills I have developed over the past five years making music—and this lends itself to computer music more than it does to me writing a song with just vocals and guitar, because of the complexity of the computer music—is the ability to hear a new part come into the mix when it sounds terrible, when it does not sound like music at all, and still know what to keep and what to change even early on, before it starts sounding good.

This skill saves a lot of time and frustration because it helps you avoid two specific failures. The first is hearing a terrible sound and immediately throwing it away, not knowing that it could be good or how to make it fit. The second is taking a sound or idea that will never work and trying to hammer it into submission. Both are forms of panic. The first is premature rejection. The second is stubborn attachment. The skill is knowing which is which before the panic sets in.

In this episode, very much in the middle of it, as I brought in two, three, four instruments, each one led to a minor panic. That sounds terrible. I do not know if this whole thing is going to collapse. The time pressure and experience get me there in the knick of time, and I am happy with the results. But the path was not smooth. Each new instrument arrived sounding wrong, like it did not belong, like it was going to ruin everything that had been built so far. And the work was not in making it sound good immediately. The work was in holding the judgment, in asking what the part was trying to do rather than grading it on how it sounded in the first three seconds.

This is not a skill I had when I started. When I started, a bad sound was just a bad sound. If something did not work immediately, I assumed it was broken and moved on. Or worse, I would spend an hour trying to fix something that had no business being in the song in the first place. The difference now is that I can hear the potential in a sound before it has arrived at its destination. I can tell the difference between a sound that is ugly because it is raw and a sound that is ugly because it is wrong.

The computer music context makes this skill more important because the gap between the raw input and the final sound is so much wider. With vocals and guitar, what you play is mostly what you get. The performance is the sound. With computer music, the performance is the selection and the processing. The initial sound is almost never the final sound. It is raw material. And the ability to look at raw material and see what it could become, without being fooled by what it currently is, is the skill that separates the producers who finish from the producers who collect unfinished projects.

I do not know if this skill transfers to other domains. I suspect it does. Any creative work that involves iteration and layering probably has an equivalent moment where something looks terrible early on and the question is whether to throw it out or trust that it will find its place. The answer is never obvious. The answer is always a judgment call. And the only way to get better at the judgment is to make the call enough times that you start to recognize the pattern.
