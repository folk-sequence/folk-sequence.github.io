Here's the game: you've got a thousand dollars, you're starting more or less from scratch, and you want a rig that can actually make records — not a wall of boxes, not a toy. What do you buy?

I went at it the way a producer thinks about a mix. Forget collecting gear. What *jobs* need doing before a guitar hits the computer? Gain and distortion. EQ. A little time and modulation. That's basically it. So the rig is three things.

The guitar. An ugly, aggressive-bite humbucker solidbody for about $279. It's not popular and it's not pretty, and that's exactly why it's cheap — and why it's a deal. I want mid-range that punches through a band, not a thick low tone that fights the bass and the kick. Bite over body. There's a whole personality you can read off a guitar's price and its looks, and the unglamorous ones are where the value hides.

Two pedals. I refuse to carry a board full of single-trick boxes. So: one Klon-style overdrive with a real active three-band EQ — which is rarer than it sounds, because most overdrives give you a single passive tone knob that only cuts highs, and I want to actually push and pull bands like an engineer would. And one analog delay that quietly also does chorus and vibrato. Two boxes, half a dozen jobs. The discipline is keeping it simple while making each box earn its place.

A tube amp. A 15-watt combo with top-boost EQ, a spring reverb, and tremolo. New it's about $1,100; used it's the $300 that made me think this whole thing was even possible. Tube, opinionated, and it does its EQ-ing across stages the way a great amp always has — a chain of choices baked into the circuit, before and after each gain stage. That layered EQ is most of why a Fender sounds like a Fender and a Vox sounds like a Vox. It isn't one knob. It's a sequence of decisions.

And here's where my day job sneaks in, because half of what an amp and an overdrive do — gain, EQ, saturation, delay, reverb — you can do in the box, cheap or free. That's literally the premise of Tackle Box, the amp emulator we make: you build the amp from gain, EQ, and saturation blocks, reorder them, and hear what each one does. No impulse responses, no mic-in-a-room theater — the honest opposite of a magic box. And if you want pedals, Manifold is a hundred of them plus a pedalboard that will host anything you drop in, each pedal also a plain VST3 or AU. None of it is required to start. It's just the same jobs, done with math instead of boxes, once the boxes have taught you what the jobs are.

You don't need any of it to begin. You need a guitar, a couple of good boxes, an amp, and the skill to use them. The skill's the only thing money can't buy — and at this budget, it's the only thing standing between you and a genuinely good record.
