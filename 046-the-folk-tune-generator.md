There is a fork in the road every time you sit down to write a song. One way leads to writing a song. The other way leads to writing a thing that writes songs. The first path is the honest path of the songwriter, the one who knows that a particular arrangement of bass notes against a particular root motion against a particular three-chord turn is the song itself and cannot be derived from anywhere else. The second path is the path of the instrument builder. You stop trying to find the right notes and start trying to build a machine that has the right notes already inside it, waiting to be shaken out. Most people are taught that the first path is real music and the second is a toy. I think that is backwards. Or at least I think the distinction is not nearly as clean as the teaching implies, because every fiddle tune that ever survived a hundred years did so by being a generator more than a composition.

The setup in Bitwig is one Stepwise sequencer feeding everything. Stepwise is a step sequencer with eight independent channels, and the channel routing is the whole point. The kick lives on channel one, where the steps that are on get sent to the drum machine and a kick fires. The bass lives on a separate device chain that listens to a filtered version of the same Stepwise output, where only certain channels are allowed through. The acoustic guitar and the banjo each take their own filtered slice. So a single physical pattern of dots on a grid drives every instrument in the session, but each instrument sees a different subset of those dots. The kick is the dense rhythm. The bass takes the kick steps too. The strings take steps that overlap with the bass and add a few more for movement.

Then comes Note Grid, which is where the real machine lives. Step Access is a module that reads the position of the incoming step inside the bar and gives you a number, and that number indexes into a scale step lookup. Counter increments every bar and indexes into a four-position chord progression, root motion only, currently the first the fifth the sixth and the fourth degree of whatever key is active. Merge combines the chord root from the Counter with the scale step from Step Access, so the bass note that fires on a given kick hit is the root of the current chord plus or minus a fifth, alternating across the bar. The guitar and banjo run the same architecture but their scale step tables contain more intervals, so they sweep through thirds and sevenths and seconds while the bass keeps the structural one and five. Nothing in the patches says G minor. Nothing in the patches names a chord. The patches only know about scale degrees and chord degrees and which channel is firing.

Naming the key is the job of Global Root Key and Global Scale, two parameters that sit one layer above the entire project and recolor every degree-based device in real time. I automated those two values to step through G minor pentatonic, then the blues minor scale, then B flat major, then back to G minor, every four bars. The same physical Stepwise pattern, the same channel routing, the same Note Grid logic, but the notes that come out of the speakers change wholesale because the entire space of legal pitches has been redefined under their feet. This is the trick that most generative attempts miss. They generate variation by randomizing the notes. The deeper move is to leave the notes alone and redefine what the notes mean.

The result is a jukebox. There is a Randomize All button on the Stepwise that scatters new dots across the eight channels and instantly produces a different tune, with the same instruments and the same harmonic motion and the same global key changes, but a fresh rhythmic and melodic identity. You hit it again, you get another tune. You hit it eighty times in an afternoon, you get eighty tunes that all sound like they came from the same band on the same evening. Some of them are better than what I would have written sitting down with a guitar. The good ones I keep. The bad ones, which are most of them, I throw away with one click. This is closer to livecoding than to composition, closer to fishing than to painting. The instrument I built does the casting. I just decide which fish are worth keeping.

It would be easy to call this cheating, and some people will. The argument is that real folk music comes from a person who has lived something and is telling you about it, and a Randomize All button has not lived anything. That argument is more romantic than accurate. Folk traditions have always relied on shared structural skeletons, the same I five six four chord motion turning up in a thousand songs, the same call and response phrasing, the same modal flavors recurring across decades because the structure itself carries the meaning. A human songwriter generating their hundredth song with a guitar is also running a probabilistic engine, just one trained on a lifetime of listening rather than declared in a patch. What I have built is a small visible version of the same engine, with the dials exposed. Whether that makes it less honest or more honest depends entirely on whether you think the work of the songwriter is to hide the machinery or to show it.
