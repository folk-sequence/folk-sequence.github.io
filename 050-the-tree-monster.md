So I went looking for a synthesizer and I found a monster instead, and I want to tell you about it, because the monster turned out to be better than the thing I was looking for.

Here's what I was actually after. I have this guitar pedal called the Meris Enzo, and it's one of my favorite boutique boxes. What it does is sort of magic the first time you hear it: you plug a guitar in, you play, and out the other end comes a synthesizer playing exactly what you played. Not a sample, not a preset triggered by your strumming — an actual oscillator-and-filter synth voice tracking your pitch in real time. No special pickup, nothing screwed into the guitar. Just the normal signal, and the pedal figures out what notes you're hitting and re-sings them as a synth.

The people who made it are worth knowing about. Meris is a small company in Los Angeles, started in 2013 by Terry Burton and Angelo Mazzocco, who'd met years earlier at Line 6. Burton had already left to co-found Strymon out of a two-bedroom apartment with his wife Jinna, and then left that to do Meris. Mazzocco's the DSP guy — the one who writes the math that turns a guitar into a synth — and his old job at Line 6 was building one-off custom rigs for people like Eddie Van Halen and The Edge. So these are not hobbyists. And the Enzo specifically came out of an obsession with old synths, the Yamaha CS-80 and the Oberheim stuff, which you can hear in it. It's a pedal made by people trying to put a polyphonic analog synth inside a stompbox and have a guitar drive it.

I wanted to see how much of that I could rebuild in Bitwig. Not because the pedal isn't great — it's right there on the floor — but because I like knowing how a thing works by trying to make it myself, badly, from parts. And the heart of the Enzo, the part that feels like magic, is the pitch tracking. The synth voices are just synth voices. The trick is the listening — the part that hears a guitar and decides, dozens of times a second, "that's an E, now that's a G," fast enough that it feels instant.

So that's where I started. I set up a chain in Bitwig that listens to the guitar, pulls the pitch out, and uses that pitch to drive an oscillator. Simple idea. One signal goes in, it branches — one branch carries the sound, another branch carries the *pitch reading*, and that reading reaches over and grabs the oscillator by the throat and tells it what note to be. And the more I wanted it to do, the more it branched. Pitch over here, volume envelope over there, a filter that opens when I dig in. Cables going everywhere. At some point I zoomed out and the device chain had sprouted into this gnarled branching thing, signal splitting and splitting, and that's when my kid walked by and called it the tree monster. And the name stuck, because that's exactly what it looked like. A tree. With a temper.

Because here's the part nobody tells you. Tracking a single clean note is easy. Tracking a *chord* is brutal. When you play two or three strings at once, the thing trying to find "the" pitch has no good answer — there are three right answers and it can only pick one — and it starts flickering between them, guessing, stuttering, leaping an octave because for one frame the math liked a harmonic better than the root. Even the Enzo, made by the Eddie Van Halen guy, basically tells you this: its monophonic mode works perfectly if you play one note at a time and goes, in the official wording, "sporadic" the instant you play two. Sporadic. That's the polite word.

My version was not polite. My version, fed a chord, would lurch and warble and chase its own tail, throwing out notes I never played, sliding around looking for solid ground it was never going to find. I sat there for a while trying to fix it. Tighten the tracking, smooth the reading, talk it down. And then I did the thing you eventually learn to do, which is stop fixing the bug and start listening to it. Because the monster, it turns out, has a sound. That panicked warble, the way it grabs a wrong note and holds it a beat too long before it bolts — fed the right slow arpeggio, that's not an error. That's a performance. It's an instrument that's a little afraid, and the fear is the good part.

So I leaned all the way in. The whole track is me feeding the tree monster careful, spaced-out notes and letting it occasionally lose its mind, and arranging the rest of the song around its little crises. The monster is the lead. Everything else — the low stuff, the pulse, the bits of guitar that stayed a guitar — is just there to give it a forest to be scared in.

I never did rebuild the Enzo. I got maybe a third of the way there and wandered off the path, which is the correct thing to do in a dark forest. The pedal does the magic trick cleanly, perfectly, every time, and that's why it costs what it costs and lives on my floor. What I built does the trick badly, and the badness has a personality, and personality is harder to buy than accuracy. Anyway. Gather round. There's a monster in the patch cables, and it sings.
