There is a machine now that knows every chord. It is called Scaler, and the third version of it arrived last spring rebuilt from the studs, and what it does is this: you give it a key, or a mood it labels Dark or Bright or Neutral, or a few notes you stumbled into, and it hands back the legal continuations, the chords that are permitted to follow, ranked and tagged and ready to drop onto a timeline beside lanes for bass and melody and arpeggiated motion. It will host other instruments inside itself now. It has, in effect, swallowed the rulebook of Western harmony and learned to recite it on demand, and it is very good, and using it feels a little like having a fluent and tireless theory tutor sitting at your shoulder who never tires of being asked the same question. I used it in this session. I also, in the same session, reached for a pedal steel, and the reason those two things sat next to each other on the same track is the reason for this essay, because they do not actually agree about what music is, and the disagreement is the most interesting thing in the room.

Start with what a chord is, because we have stopped noticing how strange the idea is. Sound is continuous. Pitch is a continuous quantity, an unbroken ribbon of frequency running from the lowest rumble you can feel in your chest to the highest needle you can almost not hear, and there are no walls in it, no rungs, no native edges. Everything between one frequency and another is also a frequency. What we did, somewhere across a few thousand years of arguing about it, was carve that ribbon into twelve named places per octave and agree to mostly stand on those places and call the spaces between them mistakes. That is the grid. The twelve-tone equal-tempered grid is the single most consequential act of compression in the history of the art, and like all compression it is a lie that buys you something: it lets a piano be tuned once and play in every key, it lets musicians who have never met agree instantly on where the notes are, it lets a chord be a noun, a thing with a name you can write down and a machine can store. Scaler is the grid made flesh. It is the grid's purest possible expression, a system that knows nothing whatsoever about the continuous ribbon underneath and everything about the legal moves between the named squares. It cannot suggest a note that has no name, because to it a note that has no name does not exist.

Now consider the pedal steel, which is the most direct possible argument against the entire premise. People think the pedal steel is a country instrument or a sad instrument, and it is both of those, but those are consequences, not causes. What it actually is, mechanically, is an instrument built so that the pitches will not hold still. You have a steel bar that you slide along the strings, which already abolishes frets and lets you stand anywhere on the ribbon you like. And then, underneath, you have the pedals and the knee levers, and what those do — this is the part that sounds like science fiction when you first understand it — is physically retune individual strings while the note is still ringing, raising one string a half step while lowering another a whole step, so that a single struck chord can be bent, voice by voice, into a different chord, with every string passing through every unnamed frequency in between on the way there. The famous crying sound, the one everyone recognizes and no one can quite describe, is not an effect laid on top of the notes. It is the sound of the notes refusing to be only one thing. It is the grid being disobeyed in real time, audibly, the instrument living in exactly the cracks that equal temperament paved over and Scaler does not know are there.

So you have, on the one track, the machine that has memorized the names, and the instrument that exists to slide between them. The temptation, the cheap and false reading, is to make this a fable about the human versus the algorithm, the warm imperfect player against the cold perfect computer, and to root for the steel and sneer at the software. I want to refuse that, because it is sentimental and it is wrong. The pedal steel is not less engineered than Scaler; it is one of the most absurdly over-engineered objects a human being can own, a small mechanical loom of rods and bell cranks under the deck that took the better part of the twentieth century to evolve, and the players who built that tradition were not fleeing from theory but burrowing deeper into it, finding the chord changes hidden inside a single sustained moment. And Scaler is not the enemy of feeling. A tool that shows you the chord you would not have found is doing the same thing a good collaborator does. The real distinction is not warm against cold. It is discrete against continuous, and that distinction is older and deeper than any technology and it cuts through everything.

Here is the claim I will actually defend. The feeling does not live in the named chords. It lives in the motion between them, and the named chords are only the points the motion is anchored to, the way a constellation is only the few bright stars and the meaning is the imaginary line your eye draws across the dark between them. Scaler can give you a flawless progression, every chord correct, every transition sanctioned, and it can be utterly dead, because correctness on the grid says nothing about the journey across the ribbon, and the journey is the whole event. The pedal steel makes this undeniable: take any two of its chords, both perfectly nameable, both things Scaler would happily hand you, and the entire emotional content of the gesture is in the half second of bend between them, in the territory that has no name and that the naming was specifically designed to ignore. We have built an enormous apparatus, theoretical and now computational, for describing the destinations, and almost no apparatus at all for describing the going, and the going is where we actually live. This is not only true of music. It is the standing error of every map we make: we name the states and forget that nobody has ever lived in a state, only in the unrepeatable transit of a life, which passes through every unnamed place on the way.

What the session was, then, was the map and the territory placed in the same room and allowed to disagree out loud. The software proposed the route, clean and lawful, the bright squares lit up in their permitted order. The steel ignored the question of which square and answered the question the grid cannot ask, which is how you get from one to the next, how slowly, through what, with how much reluctance to arrive. Use the machine. I do, gladly, and you should, and pretending otherwise is a pose. But know what it is for. It is a magnificent gazetteer of the places that have names. The thing worth making is almost never one of those places. It is the slide, the bend, the note caught halfway and unwilling to land, the crying that is just a pitch declining to be told which of the twelve it has decided to be.
